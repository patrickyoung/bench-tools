# Composition direction

Translate the request into a picture-level decision before placing objects:
identify the primary read, the supporting read, and what should remain quiet.
Choose a focal zone, a counterweight or release, and an eye path. Use the
request's aspect ratio and stated style as constraints; do not force centered,
asymmetric, portrait, or landscape compositions.

Build in three passes:

1. Establish the largest value masses and preserve clear breathing room around
   the primary silhouette.
2. Add overlapping support forms that clarify scale, depth, or narrative rather
   than merely filling space.
3. Reserve small accents, contour cuts, and directional texture for the focal
   subject and the intended eye path. Repeated marks should follow the form,
   growth, or movement they describe.

Use contrast deliberately. A limited accent color, light edge, or dark contour
should have a job: attract attention, separate planes, or explain material.
Make depth through overlap, relative scale, value grouping, and directional
planes; do not compensate for a weak composition with ornament.

Before authoring, mentally test the image at thumbnail scale: the main
silhouette should read, secondary elements should not compete, and open space
should look intentional. Then name plan layers by visual role and construct
them back-to-front.

This is a method, not a layout library. Never reuse a prior scene, object
placement, palette, object count, or path geometry as a default. A brief may
call for symmetry, dense detail, or an off-center focal point; honor that
instruction when it improves the requested image. Structural checks establish
provenance and rendering, not composition quality, so record honest review
limits in the design notes.
